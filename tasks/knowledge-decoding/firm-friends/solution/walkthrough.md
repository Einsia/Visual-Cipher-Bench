# Firm Friends — Walkthrough

1. At the top of page 1, the example shows a key and a bone joined by a line. The key's circle carries a rightward arrow and the bone's a downward arrow, and the little L-shaped grid has a three-cell across entry and a four-cell down entry that share their last cell. KEY and BONE themselves do not fit: they have the wrong lengths and their final letters Y and E differ. Exchanging the first written letters (BEY/KONE) fails the same way. Exchanging the initial consonant sounds and respelling gives Bee and Cone: three and four letters, sharing the final E. This determines the operation: pair two picture names, exchange their initial consonant sounds, and put each result into the entry belonging to its original word ending. It does not say to reverse a word or to put an intact compound into one cell.

2. Page 2 shows the twelve pictures arranged in a ring, each beside its own small circle, and asks that paired circles be joined by straight lines that do not cross. So the twelve pictures form six reciprocal pairs, and the pairing must be non-crossing around the ring. Going round from the top, the pictures are a saw, a spool of wire, a hoe, an animal hide, a window with its sill pointed out, a light bulb, a rack of shelves, a bead on a string, rain, a sack of Rye, falling leaves (Fall), and a treasure chest of loot. Unlike the example, no picture says whether its result goes across or down.

3. On page 1, the thick boundaries separate entries, and a crossing is one shared letter. The four horizontal runs fixed by vertical walls are across entries: four cells in the top row, five cells in the second row, four cells at the right end of the third row, and four cells in the row below the grey one. All other cells belong to down entries. Inside the single-width columns some horizontal lines are omitted; as the note says, a black stub marks the end of an entry and a grey stub a continuation, so those down entries have to be sized by the words that fit. The grey row has a question mark instead of a picture. It is the requested word, read from left to right, and each of its eight cells lies in a different down entry, so eight of the twelve results go down.

4. Keep both members of a tentative pair together. For example, the bulb and the rain can give Right and Lane. Right needs five cells and Lane four; Right fits the five-cell across run in the second row, and Lane fits the fourth column from the top row down to the grey row, sharing its L with the last letter of the top-row entry. A spelling such as Lain has the same sound, so do not discard it merely because Lane is familiar; leave that possibility until the whole grid and the grey word are checked. The output from one picture must retain that picture's ending sound. Bulb and rain sit three places apart on the ring with the bead and the rack between them, so those two must pair with each other.

5. The Rye sack has a three-letter name but its partner must give a four-letter answer. That visible mismatch rules out merely swapping the first written letters. Pairing Rye with the hoe produces High and Row, which fit: High is the four-cell across run on the third row, and Row runs down from the R of Right. Rye and hoe lie opposite each other on the right-hand arc, enclosing the hide, sill, bulb, rack, bead and rain, which must then pair among themselves without crossing.

6. The window pointer identifies the sill; the animal skin is a hide. Exchanging their beginnings gives Hill and Side. Both go down: Hill starts at the fourth letter H of Right and crosses the I of High, and Side runs down the third column, starting just below the top-row run. The third letter L of Hill and the third letter D of Side enter the grey row. Sill and Hide cannot also donate their first sounds to other pictures, since the example exchanges a pair in both directions. Keep the crossing letters when checking possible alternative partners.

7. The round pierced object on a string is a bead, and the shelving is a rack. They give Read (or the homophone Reed) and Back. Reed is the four-cell across run below the grey row; its first R is the third letter of the vertical Fire entry, and its second E meets the final E of Side. Back runs down from just below the I of Right, through the grey row. The interior E/A distinction in Read/Reed is unchecked and need not be forced; it does not lie in the grey answer row.

8. The coil can be called wire, and the falling-leaves scene gives fall. Wire/Fall produces Fire/Wall. Wall fills the top row; Fire runs down the second column, crossing the shaded row at I and the start of Read/Reed at R. Finally, the hand saw and the treasure, which are neighbours at the top of the ring, can be named Saw/Loot, giving Law/Suit. Suit runs down the left-hand column and fills the first grey cell with S; Law runs down from the grey row and fills the seventh with L. The paired use matters: choosing a sound for each entry independently would permit other apparently coherent grey words.

9. Check the entire fill, keeping reciprocal partners rather than choosing unrelated initials: Rye↔Hoe, Light↔Rain, Sill↔Hide, Bead↔Rack, Wire↔Fall, Saw↔Loot. The six joining lines (saw–loot, wire–fall, hoe–rye, hide–sill, bulb–rain, rack–bead) are nested and do not cross. All picture clues are used once, every cell is covered, and the shared cells agree. The grey row receives S from Suit, I from Fire, D from Side, E from Lane, W from Row, A from Back, L from Law and L from Hill. Read it left to right: **SIDEWALL**.

These steps give one ordinary-word completion; they do not assume every internal spelling is unique. Read/Reed and pronunciation-equivalent names in the Wall slot can vary without changing the answer. Other tentative pairings must be tested against all crossings, the non-crossing lines, and the requirement that the grey row forms an English word. The joined circles in the example require reciprocal pairs, so a proposed filling that uses independent, unreturned sound donations does not follow the example.

**Answer: SIDEWALL**
