# Track Meet — Walkthrough

Read down the page, following the arrows at the left. Each column keeps a word from one row to the next. The two arrowheads on a horizontal bracket select the portions that trade places. The question mark in the third box of the bottom row marks the requested word.

Start with the upper example. Its inputs are a bed, the word RASH and coal; only BASH and ROLE are printed at the bottom, and the middle row and the last box are left for you to fill. The first bracket joins the right halves of the first two boxes. BED and RASH trade their right portions: B | ED and R | ASH become BASH, which is printed below the first column, and a word sounding like “red.” COAL is untouched for now.

The next bracket joins the right portions of the middle word and COAL, and the middle column ends as ROLE. A literal letter exchange cannot explain this, but spoken portions can: “r” plus the end of COAL, sounding like “ole,” gives ROLE, and COAL's leading “k” takes the middle word's ending. If the middle word were RED, the third column would become “ked,” which is not a word. The middle word must therefore be READ: it first arrives with the pronunciation “red,” then uses its pronunciation “reed.” This preserves the spelling between rows, and the third box becomes “k” plus “eed,” KEYED. The full example is BED, RASH, COAL → BASH, READ, COAL → BASH, ROLE, KEYED. The two exchanges together reveal that each exchange works on sounds, that a word can be respelled when it is made, and that a retained spelling can later use another ordinary pronunciation.

Now consider the four lower drawings: a ribbon tied into a bow, a gemstone ring, a group of trees, and a table. RIBBON, RING, FOREST and TABLE are useful names. BOW and WOODS are natural alternatives to try; they must also satisfy the same successive exchanges, so test them rather than ruling them out in advance.

The first lower bracket joins the left portions of the ribbon and ring columns. Split spoken RIBBON after the “rib” sound and RING after its initial “r.” Trading those leading sounds makes RUN and RIBBING. Ordinary word stress belongs to the resulting whole words. The other two columns remain FOREST and TABLE.

The second bracket joins the right portions of the first and third columns. Split RUN after its initial “r,” and FOREST after “for.” Trading the remaining sounds produces RUST and FOREIGN. The row is now RUST, RIBBING, FOREIGN, TABLE. These are newly chosen divisions of the current words; the original divisions need not persist.

The third bracket joins the right portion of FOREIGN with the left portion of TABLE. Trading FOREIGN's ending, sounding like “un,” with TABLE's initial “t” gives a word sounding like FORT and the word UNABLE. FORT and FORTE are both spellings to keep in mind for that sound; the final exchange must decide between them.

The last bracket joins the left portion of RUST with the right portion of that third-column word. FORTE has both the pronunciation “fort” and the familiar pronunciation “for-tay.” With the latter, split it before the final “ay” sound, and split RUST before its final “t.” Trading “rus” and “ay” gives EIGHT and FORTRESS. FORT alone has no final “ay” to exchange. As in the example's READ, the spelling FORTE is retained while its pronunciation changes.

The final row can read EIGHT, RIBBING, FORTRESS, UNABLE. ATE is another spelling of the first sound, but that box is not marked. The question mark is in the third column, so the answer is **FORTRESS**.

**Answer: FORTRESS**
